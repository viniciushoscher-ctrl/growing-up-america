## 3:48 · The draft

**11. [REGISTER AT 18]** · fotos: `Selective Service draft board office` (3)  
In nineteen fifty-seven, America had a peacetime draft. Every young man had to register with the Selective Service when he turned eighteen. You walked into the local board office, filled out a card, and they gave you a small paper card of your own, which you were supposed to carry with you at all times.

**12. [ENLIST OR WAIT]** · fotos: `1950s military enlistment recruits` (3)  
You might not be called right away. Some young men were deferred for college, or for farm work, or for a family that depended on them. There was another option, too. Under a law passed in nineteen fifty-five, a young man could join the reserves, serve around six months of active duty, and then spend the next several years training on weekends and in the summer. For a boy who wanted to start a job or marry his sweetheart, that was often the choice his father recommended.

A young couple with a little money saved, and a veteran's loan if the husband had served, could buy a small house in one of the new subdivisions. ## 18:47 · The car you drove

**50. [1957: CHROME AND FINS]** · fotos: `1957 Chevrolet Bel Air` (3)  
Whether you were single or married, you needed a car. And in nineteen fifty-seven, American cars were at their most spectacular. Long, low, two-toned, covered in chrome, and growing fins on the back like rocket ships.

**51. [THE '57 CHEVY]** · fotos: `1957 Chevrolet Bel Air convertible` (3)  
The car of the year, for many people then and now, was the nineteen fifty-seven Chevrolet Bel Air. Turquoise and white, or red and white, with those sharp little fins and that gleaming grille. **54. [FORD VS CHEVY]** · fotos: `1950s mechanic car engine garage` (3)  
For many young men, the car was also their workshop. On Saturday afternoons, you'd find them in driveways and gas station bays all over America, with the hood up and their heads inside the engine. Your own first car was probably used. A forty-nine Ford. A fifty-one Chevy with a cracked dashboard. You learned to change the oil yourself, to fix a flat on the side of the road, and to push it down the hill to get it started on a cold morning. **56. [NO SEAT BELTS, WINDOWS DOWN]** · fotos: `1950s highway road trip car` (3)  
There were no seat belts in most cars, and nobody thought to ask for them. There was no air conditioning, either, so in summer you drove with all the windows down and your elbow resting on the door. For many young people, the newest thing in their hands was a small transistor radio. For the first time, you could carry the music with you. To the beach, to the park, to your bedroom late at night. The radio was no longer the family's. It was yours.

The boys wore their hair in crew cuts, or, if they were daring, swept back with grease into a ducktail. They wore white T-shirts and blue jeans rolled at the cuff. ## 23:53 · The autumn of 1957

**64. [THE AUTUMN OF 1957]** · fotos: `1957 newspaper headlines` (3)  
If you were eighteen in nineteen fifty-seven, you remember that autumn, even if you don't remember why. Because in just a few weeks, three things happened that changed the country you were stepping into.

**65. [SEPTEMBER 1957: LITTLE ROCK]** · fotos: `Little Rock Central High School 1957` (3)  
In September, in Little Rock, Arkansas, nine Black teenagers tried to enter Central High School. They were your age, or close to it. Crowds screamed at them. The governor sent the National Guard to keep them out. And President Eisenhower sent soldiers of the hundred and first Airborne Division to walk them through the door.

**66. [THE LITTLE ROCK NINE]** · fotos: `Little Rock Nine 1957 soldiers` (3)  
You watched it on the evening news. Kids your own age, carrying their schoolbooks, walking past bayonets. Whatever you thought about it then, you never forgot those pictures.

**67. [OCTOBER 4, 1957: SPUTNIK]** · fotos: `Sputnik 1 satellite` (3)  
Then, on October fourth, nineteen fifty-seven, the Soviet Union launched Sputnik, the first artificial satellite. It circled the Earth every ninety-six minutes, sending out a little beep that radio operators could hear all over the world. Some people stood in their yards at night trying to spot it.

**68. [THE SAME NIGHT: LEAVE IT TO BEAVER]** · fotos: `1950s family watching television` (3)  
And here's a strange coincidence. That very same evening, a gentle new family comedy premiered on television. It was called Leave It to Beaver. So on the same night, Americans saw the most comforting picture of family life ever put on a screen, and heard that the Soviets had beaten them into space.

**69. [A CONFUSING MESSAGE]** · fotos: `1950s television news broadcast` (3)  
For a young person, it was a confusing message. On one channel, a perfect family in a perfect house. On another, a news anchor talking about Soviet missiles. Many of you remember your parents' faces that week, quieter than usual, and the feeling that the world had suddenly become a little more dangerous.

**70. [THE SPACE RACE]** · fotos: `1950s college students science laboratory` (3)  
Overnight, everything changed in the schools. Science and mathematics became a national priority. Within a year, Congress passed a law to pay for science education and student loans. If you were thinking about college, suddenly the government wanted very much for you to go.

**71. [JANUARY 1958: EXPLORER 1]** · fotos: `Explorer 1 satellite launch 1958` (3)  
America tried to answer quickly. On December sixth, nineteen fifty-seven, the Navy's Vanguard rocket rose about four feet off the launch pad, and exploded on live television. The newspapers mocked it. Some called it Flopnik. But less than two months later, on January thirty-first, nineteen fifty-eight, America's first satellite, Explorer One, reached orbit.

**72. [1957: THE ASIAN FLU]** · fotos: `1957 influenza hospital` (3)  
And that same autumn, a new strain of influenza swept the country. People called it the Asian flu. Schools emptied, offices ran short, and according to the C D C, about one hundred sixteen thousand Americans died from it over the next year. Many of you remember being sick in bed for a week, or a classmate who never came back.


## 26:49 · What adulthood really meant

**73. [THE RECESSION OF 1958]** · fotos: `1950s unemployment office line` (3)  
Then, as if all that wasn't enough, the economy slowed down. The recession of nineteen fifty-seven and fifty-eight hit the factory towns hard. According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, unemployment climbed above seven percent by the summer of nineteen fifty-eight. Young workers, the last ones hired, were often the first ones let go.

**74. [RESPONSIBILITY, ALL AT ONCE]** · fotos: `1950s young couple apartment` (3)  
So adulthood, for an eighteen-year-old in nineteen fifty-seven, wasn't only freedom. It was responsibility, arriving all at once. A paycheck that had to stretch. A draft card in your wallet. A wedding ring, maybe, and a baby on the way. A world that seemed to change every time you turned on the news.

**75. [MOVING BACK HOME]** · fotos: `1950s young family porch` (3)  
A young husband who lost his job at the plant in nineteen fifty-eight didn't have much of a safety net. There was unemployment insurance for a while, and a few odd jobs, and family who helped where they could. Many young couples moved back in with their parents for a few months. Nobody talked about it much, but a lot of people did it.

**76. [TOMORROW WOULD BE BETTER]** · fotos: `1950s neighbors suburb street` (3)  
And yet, ask almost anyone who lived it, and they'll tell you there was something good about it too. You knew your neighbors. Your boss knew your name. A hard-working young person, with a high school diploma, could get a job, buy a car, start a family, and believe that tomorrow would be better than today. For a great many of you, it was.

**77. [YOU BELONGED]** · fotos: `1950s church supper community hall` (3)
