## 1:34 · The colors

Let's start with the color of the room itself. Today, most kitchens are white, or gray, or stainless steel. But in nineteen sixty-two, the American kitchen was bursting with color. Turquoise. Pink. Sunny yellow. Mint green. And a warm, coppery brown that the manufacturers called coppertone.

**6. [COLOR SOLD THE WHOLE KITCHEN]** · fotos: `pink kitchen 1950s retro` (3)  
The color wasn't just on the walls. It was on the refrigerator, the stove, the countertop, and sometimes even the telephone. Appliance makers had learned that a housewife who bought a pink stove would eventually want a pink refrigerator to match it. Color was a way of selling the whole kitchen, one piece at a time.

**7. [AVOCADO CAME LATER]** · fotos: `avocado green kitchen appliances` (3)  
Here's the twist that a lot of people get wrong. The famous avocado green and harvest gold that people picture when they think of a retro kitchen didn't really arrive until later in the decade. In nineteen sixty-two, the palette was still the soft pastels of the fifties. Avocado was coming. But it wasn't there yet.

**8. [LINOLEUM AND FORMICA]** · fotos: `linoleum floor checkerboard kitchen` (3)  
On the floor was linoleum, or vinyl tile, often laid in a checkerboard pattern. On the counters, Formica, sometimes in a pattern of little boomerangs or stars. It was all designed for one thing. To wipe clean in a single pass, because the woman of the house was expected to keep it spotless.

At the window hung café curtains, covering just the bottom half, in gingham checks or a print of teapots, roosters, or little bunches of cherries. ## 3:23 · The kitchen table

**10. [THE DINETTE SET]** · fotos: `chrome dinette set Formica table` (3)  
In the middle of the room stood the dinette set. A table with a Formica top and a shiny chrome edge, and four or six chairs upholstered in vinyl. Red, or yellow, or gray with a pattern of little sparkles in it. In summer, those chairs stuck to the backs of your legs. In winter, they were cold.

**11. [UNDER THE TABLE]** · fotos: `vintage kitchen table chairs` (3)  
Under that table, generations of children built forts, hid from their brothers, and tied their sisters' shoelaces together. The underside was where the chewing gum went when nobody was looking. And if you ever got down there again today, I'd bet the gum would still be there.

**12. [WHERE EVERYTHING HAPPENED]** · fotos: `1960s family breakfast kitchen table` (3)  
That table was where everything happened. Breakfast before school. Homework in the afternoon. Your father reading the evening paper. Your mother paying the bills, writing a grocery list, or talking on the phone with her sister. And late at night, the grown-ups sitting with coffee, talking quietly about things the children weren't supposed to hear.

**13. [THE ASHTRAY ON THE TABLE]** · fotos: `vintage glass ashtray` (3)  
And in the middle of the table, almost always, sat an ashtray. Glass, or heavy ceramic, sometimes a souvenir from a vacation. In nineteen sixty-two, a large share of American adults smoked, and smoking at the kitchen table was as normal as drinking coffee there. Two years later, the Surgeon General's report would begin to change that. But not yet.

**14. [THE STEP STOOL]** · fotos: `vintage kitchen step stool` (3)  
Next to the table, there was often a little step stool, the kind with steps that folded out from underneath the seat. It was a chair, a ladder, and a place for a small child to sit and watch their mother cook, all in one. Somebody in your family probably still has one.


## 5:15 · The stove

**15. [THE STOVE]** · fotos: `vintage gas stove kitchen` (3)  
Now turn around, and look at the stove. It might have been gas, with a little blue pilot light that never went out, and a box of wooden matches on a shelf nearby just in case. Or it might have been electric, with coiled burners that glowed orange. Either way, it was big, white or colored to match, and it was the busiest thing in the house.

**16. [THE GREASE CAN]** · fotos: `novelty salt and pepper shakers` (3)  
On the back of the stove sat the salt and pepper shakers, often shaped like something silly. A pair of chickens. Two little Dutch children. A cowboy boot. And next to them, a can or a jar for bacon grease. Every drop was poured off and saved, to fry eggs the next morning, or to season the green beans.

**17. [EVERY STOVE HAD ITS QUIRKS]** · fotos: `1950s electric range stove` (3)  
Some of the fancier stoves had a built-in griddle in the middle for pancakes, a clock with a timer on the back panel, and even a second oven. Your mother knew every quirk of hers. Which burner ran hot. Which corner of the oven burned the cookies. She adjusted for all of it without ever opening a manual.


## 6:30 · The refrigerator

**18. [THE TINY FREEZER]** · fotos: `vintage refrigerator 1950s` (3)  
Now let's open the refrigerator. In nineteen sixty-two, it was probably a single tall door, with rounded edges and a big chrome handle. And at the top, inside, there was a tiny freezer compartment, barely big enough for two ice cube trays and a box of frozen peas.

**19. [DEFROSTING DAY]** · fotos: `defrosting freezer ice` (3)  
And that freezer compartment had a problem. Frost. In many homes, the frost slowly grew thicker and thicker, until the freezer door wouldn't close. So every few weeks, somebody had to defrost it. You unplugged the refrigerator, emptied it out, and put pans of hot water inside to melt the ice. Sometimes your father got impatient and chipped at it with a knife.

**20. [FROST-FREE WAS A LUXURY]** · fotos: `1960s refrigerator advertisement` (3)  
Frost-free refrigerators were already on the market, and they were one of the great luxuries of the age. But they cost more, and plenty of families kept the old one running for years. If you ever chipped ice out of a freezer with a butter knife, you know exactly what I mean.

**21. [THE ALUMINUM ICE TRAY]** · fotos: `aluminum ice cube tray lever` (3)  
Then there were the ice cube trays. Made of aluminum, with a metal grid inside and a lever on top. You pulled the lever, and the grid cracked the ice loose, mostly. Half the cubes broke into pieces. And if your fingers were wet, they'd stick to the cold metal for a terrifying second.

**22. [NOTHING WAS WASTED]** · fotos: `glass milk bottles vintage` (3)  
Inside the refrigerator, you'd find a glass bottle of milk, delivered that morning, and a stick of real butter in a covered dish. A jar of bacon grease sat on the stove, not in the fridge, because nobody wasted grease. And there was always a bowl of leftovers covered with a plate, or with waxed paper and a rubber band.

**23. [NO FRIDGE MAGNETS YET]** · fotos: `vintage refrigerator door kitchen` (3)  
And here's something you won't find on that refrigerator door. Magnets. The little decorative fridge magnets that cover kitchens today didn't become common until the nineteen seventies. In nineteen sixty-two, the children's drawings and the school lunch menu were held up with tape, or tucked into the frame of a mirror.


## 8:45 · The telephone on the wall

**24. [THE WALL PHONE]** · fotos: `rotary wall telephone kitchen` (3)  
On the kitchen wall hung the telephone. Black, or white, or in a color to match the room. It had a rotary dial, and it was rented, not owned, from the telephone company. Most families had only one, and the kitchen was the natural place for it, because that's where Mother spent most of her day.

**25. [THE LONG CURLY CORD]** · fotos: `telephone curly cord vintage` (3)  
And it had a cord. A long, curly cord, that over the years stretched longer and longer, as your mother walked from the stove to the sink to the table while she talked. Teenagers stretched it all the way into the pantry and shut the door, hoping for a little privacy. The cord always ended up hopelessly tangled.

**26. [THE PRINCESS PHONE]** · fotos: `Princess telephone Bell` (3)  
The newest telephone, the one every teenage girl wanted for her bedroom, was the Princess phone. Bell introduced it in nineteen fifty-nine. It was small and slim, came in soft colors, and had a dial that lit up at night. The advertisements said, it's little, it's lovely, it lights.

**27. [BUTTERFIELD 8]** · fotos: `vintage telephone dial exchange` (3)  
Next to the kitchen phone, there was usually a little pad of paper, a pencil on a string, and a list of important numbers taped to the wall. The doctor. The pharmacy. Grandma. And telephone numbers in those days often began with letters, an exchange name like Pennsylvania six or Butterfield eight. Many of you can still recite your childhood number.

**28. [LONG DISTANCE MEANT NEWS]** · fotos: `vintage telephone woman kitchen` (3)  
Long distance calls were expensive, so they were saved for birthdays, holidays and bad news. When the phone rang late at night, everybody's heart jumped. And when Grandma called from another state, the whole family lined up to say a quick hello, while somebody watched the clock on the wall.


## 10:37 · The countertop

**29. [THE PERCOLATOR]** · fotos: `electric coffee percolator vintage` (3)  
Now let's look along the counter. First, the coffee. In nineteen sixty-two, nobody had a drip coffee maker. The famous Mr. Coffee machine wouldn't arrive until nineteen seventy-two. Instead, there was the percolator, a tall metal pot with a little glass knob on top.

**30. [BLUB, BLUB, BLUB]** · fotos: `percolator coffee pot glass top` (3)  
As the coffee heated, it bubbled up into that glass knob, again and again, with a sound like a little drum. Blub. Blub. Blub. You could tell how strong it was getting by the color in the glass. That sound, and that smell, meant morning in millions of American homes.

**31. [FLOUR, SUGAR, COFFEE, TEA]** · fotos: `vintage kitchen canister set` (3)  
Next to it stood the canister set. Four matching containers, from biggest to smallest, labeled flour, sugar, coffee, and tea. Some were metal, painted with flowers. Some were ceramic, or glass with red lids. And very often, the one labeled tea actually held something else, like rubber bands or the money for the milkman.

**32. [THE BREAD BOX]** · fotos: `vintage bread box metal` (3)  
There was a bread box, made of metal with a roll-top door, keeping the loaf of white bread soft for a few extra days. And a cookie jar, shaped like a bear, or an owl, or a little cottage. The lid always made a loud clink when you lifted it, which is why so many kids got caught.

**33. [THE MIXMASTER]** · fotos: `Sunbeam Mixmaster vintage mixer` (3)  
And in pride of place, there was the electric mixer. Very often, a Sunbeam Mixmaster, with two glass bowls that turned on a little platform, and two beaters that whirred away. For decades, it was one of the most common wedding presents in America. And when your mother was done, you got to lick the beaters.

**34. [THE ELECTRIC CAN OPENER]** · fotos: `electric can opener vintage` (3)  
The newest gadget on many counters was an electric can opener. They had begun appearing in the late nineteen fifties, and by the early sixties, they were a status symbol. You set the can in place, pressed the lever, and the can turned all by itself with a satisfying grinding hum. People demonstrated them to visitors.

**35. [THE CHROME TOASTER]** · fotos: `chrome toaster vintage` (3)  
And then there was the toaster. Chrome, rounded and heavy, so shiny you could see your face stretched across the side like a funhouse mirror. It had a little lever on the side, and a dial for light or dark that nobody ever trusted. Somebody always ended up scraping burnt toast over the sink with a butter knife.


## 13:12 · The drawers and cupboards

**36. [THE CHURCH KEY]** · fotos: `church key can opener` (3)  
Now let's open the drawers. In the first one, the silverware, and somewhere in the back, a church key. That's what everyone called the flat metal opener with a pointed end. Because in nineteen sixty-two, you couldn't just pull a tab to open a can of soda or juice. You had to punch two triangle holes in the top.

**37. [1962: THE PULL TAB]** · fotos: `pull tab beverage can` (3)  
And here's a little piece of history. That very year, nineteen sixty-two, a man named Ermal Fraze in Dayton, Ohio, patented the pull-tab top, after he'd been stuck at a picnic without a church key. Iron City Beer in Pittsburgh was one of the first to use it. Within a few years, the church key started to disappear from American drawers.

**38. [THE JUNK DRAWER]** · fotos: `kitchen drawer utensils vintage` (3)  
Then there was the junk drawer. Every kitchen had one. String, twist ties, birthday candles, a screwdriver, a flashlight that didn't work, keys to locks nobody could identify, and a stack of trading stamps waiting to be licked and pasted into their books.

**39. [PYREX BOWLS]** · fotos: `Pyrex mixing bowls vintage` (3)  
In the cupboards, you'd find the bowls. And in nineteen sixty-two, there's a very good chance they were Pyrex. Opaque glass mixing bowls in bright colors, primary red, yellow, green and blue, sized to nest one inside the other. Today, collectors pay real money for a full set. Back then, they were simply what you mixed the cake in.

**40. [CORNING WARE]** · fotos: `Corning Ware Blue Cornflower` (3)  
Next to them, maybe, a white casserole dish with little blue flowers on the side. That was Corning Ware, introduced in nineteen fifty-eight. It was made from a material developed from research into rocket nose cones, and you could take it straight from the freezer to the oven. The blue cornflower pattern became one of the most familiar designs in American kitchens.

**41. [THE CAST IRON SKILLET]** · fotos: `cast iron skillet vintage` (3)  
And in the bottom cupboard, a heavy cast iron skillet, black and seasoned over many years, that might have belonged to your grandmother. Next to it, an aluminum pressure cooker, which hissed and rattled on the stove, and made every child in the house keep a careful distance.

**42. [THE EGG BEATER AND THE SIFTER]** · fotos: `vintage hand egg beater kitchen` (3)  
And in the drawer next to the stove lived the hand tools. A hand-cranked egg beater, with two little whisks that spun when you turned the handle. A flour sifter with a squeaky trigger. A wooden spoon worn flat on one side. A potato masher. And in some homes, a metal meat grinder that clamped onto the edge of the table.

**43. [BUILT TO LAST]** · fotos: `vintage kitchen utensils` (3)  
Many of these tools had no motor at all, and some of them had been wedding gifts twenty years before. They were heavy, simple, and almost impossible to break. If you inherited one, there's a fair chance it still works perfectly today, while the gadgets your children bought last year have already gone in the trash.


## 16:16 · The Tupperware party

**44. [THE TUPPERWARE PARTY]** · fotos: `Tupperware containers vintage` (3)  
Now, one of the most important things in your mother's kitchen didn't come from a store at all. It came from a party. A Tupperware party. Tupperware's plastic containers, with their famous airtight seal, were sold almost entirely through home parties, hosted by women for their friends and neighbors.

**45. [BURP THE LID]** · fotos: `1950s women party living room` (3)  
The hostess served coffee and cake. A saleswoman demonstrated the products, sometimes tossing a sealed bowl full of liquid across the room to prove it wouldn't leak. You burped the lid, to push out the air, and everybody laughed. And at the end, everyone placed an order, and the hostess got a gift for having the party.

**46. [MORE THAN PLASTIC BOWLS]** · fotos: `1960s housewives social gathering` (3)  
For many women, those parties were about much more than plastic bowls. They were a social life, a reason to dress up, and for some, a real job and an income of their own, at a time when that wasn't easy to find. A lot of women built careers out of a living room full of neighbors.

**47. [STILL IN YOUR CUPBOARD]** · fotos: `vintage plastic kitchen containers` (3)  
And if you look in your own kitchen cupboard right now, there's a decent chance you'll find one of those containers still there. Faded a little, maybe missing its lid. But still doing its job, sixty years later.


## 17:38 · The pantry

**48. [THE PANTRY]** · fotos: `vintage pantry shelves canned goods` (3)  
Off the kitchen, in many homes, there was a pantry, or at least a deep cupboard with shelves to the ceiling. It was lined with cans. Soup, peaches, corn, evaporated milk, and coffee in tall metal cans that needed a key to open. There was a sack of potatoes on the floor, and an onion bin, and always more than one box of crackers.

**49. [CANNING SEASON]** · fotos: `Mason jars home canning` (3)  
And in late summer, in a great many kitchens, there was canning. Your mother, maybe with your grandmother beside her, spent long, hot days putting up tomatoes, peaches, green beans, pickles and jam in glass Mason jars. The kitchen was steaming. The jars sat cooling on towels, and every few minutes, one would make a little pop as the lid sealed.

**50. [SUMMER IN A JAR]** · fotos: `home canned preserves shelves basement` (3)  
Those jars went down to the basement shelves, and all winter long you ate summer. A jar of peaches in January tasted like July. Today, people pay a lot of money for something called artisanal preserves. In nineteen sixty-two, your mother just called it putting up food, and she'd have laughed at the price.


## 18:52 · What was for dinner

**51. [MEAT, POTATOES, VEGETABLE]** · fotos: `1960s family dinner meal` (3)  
So what came out of that kitchen? In nineteen sixty-two, dinner was usually meat, a starch and a vegetable. Pot roast. Meatloaf. Pork chops. Chicken on Sunday. Mashed potatoes with butter. Green beans, or peas, very often from a can. And on Friday, in many homes, fish.

**52. [OPEN ONE CAN OF SOUP]** · fotos: `canned food grocery shelves vintage` (3)  
Canned and frozen food felt modern, and modern was good. Cream of mushroom soup went into everything. Casseroles topped with crushed potato chips or fried onions. Tuna noodle casserole on a weeknight. Green bean casserole at Thanksgiving. A whole generation of recipes started with the words, open one can of soup.

**53. [DINNER'S READY]** · fotos: `1960s mother calling children back door` (3)  
When dinner was ready, your mother didn't text anybody. She opened the back door and called your name, once, twice, and if you didn't come the third time, she used your middle name. Then you knew you were in trouble. Some mothers rang a bell. Some fathers whistled. Every child knew their family's call.

**54. [THE JELL-O MOLD]** · fotos: `gelatin mold salad vintage` (3)  
And then there was gelatin. Jell-O was everywhere. Not just for dessert, but in salads, molded into rings and stars in copper or aluminum molds, with fruit, or vegetables, or even meat suspended inside it. To your grandchildren, it sounds strange. In nineteen sixty-two, it was elegant.

**55. [THE TV DINNER]** · fotos: `TV dinner aluminum tray` (3)  
For busy nights, there was the TV dinner. Swanson had made them famous in the nineteen fifties. An aluminum tray with three compartments. Turkey, mashed potatoes and peas. You heated it in the oven for about twenty-five minutes, because there was no microwave. And you ate it on a folding metal TV tray in front of the set.

**56. [THE TV TRAY]** · fotos: `folding metal TV tray vintage` (3)  
The TV tray itself deserves a moment. It was a thin metal tray, painted with flowers or a woodgrain pattern, on folding legs that wobbled if you breathed on them. Families had sets of four, kept in a little rack in the corner. Bring them out, and suddenly dinner in front of the television felt like a special occasion.

**57. [NO MICROWAVE YET]** · fotos: `vintage gas stove oven` (3)  
And that's another thing that kitchen didn't have. A microwave oven. The first countertop microwave for the home wouldn't arrive until nineteen sixty-seven. In nineteen sixty-two, everything was heated in the oven or on the stove, and leftovers took twenty minutes, not two.

**58. [CEREAL AND TANG]** · fotos: `breakfast cereal boxes vintage` (3)  
In the cupboard, there was a box of cereal with a toy at the bottom that the children fought over. And maybe a jar of Tang, the orange drink powder that came out in nineteen fifty-nine. A few years later, it would fly into space with NASA astronauts, and sales would take off with it.

**59. [PACKING THE LUNCHBOXES]** · fotos: `vintage metal lunch box` (3)  
Every school morning, the kitchen counter turned into a little assembly line. Bread laid out in rows. Peanut butter and jelly, or bologna with mustard. Each sandwich wrapped in waxed paper, folded at the corners. An apple, a cookie, and a thermos, all packed into a metal lunchbox with a picture on the side, or a brown paper bag with your name written on it.

**60. [SATURDAY BAKING]** · fotos: `homemade pie baking kitchen` (3)  
On Saturday mornings, the kitchen often filled with the smell of baking. Bread for the week, cinnamon rolls, a pie, or a cake for Sunday. A cake made from scratch was still common, though cake mixes in a box were becoming more popular every year. Some of them told you to add a fresh egg yourself, so the baker would feel like she'd really made it.

**61. [THE CAKE STAND]** · fotos: `glass cake stand dome vintage` (3)  
And on special occasions, there was a homemade layer cake on a glass cake stand with a domed cover. It sat on the counter, in full view, where everybody could admire it and nobody was allowed to touch it until after dinner. That cake stand might be the most tempting object in the history of the American kitchen.


## 22:59 · The recipe box and the cookbook

**62. [THE RECIPE BOX]** · fotos: `vintage recipe box index cards` (3)  
On the shelf, or on top of the refrigerator, sat a small metal or wooden box full of index cards. The recipe box. Every card was handwritten, many of them by women who are long gone now. Grandma's pie crust. Aunt Ruth's potato salad. A neighbor's coffee cake, with her name at the top.

**63. [HAROLD'S FAVORITE]** · fotos: `handwritten recipe card` (3)  
Those cards were splattered with butter and vanilla, and stained with coffee rings. Some had little notes in the margins. Use less sugar. Bake ten minutes longer. Harold's favorite. If you still have your mother's recipe box, you know that it's one of the most precious things you own.

**64. [BETTY CROCKER NEVER EXISTED]** · fotos: `vintage cookbook kitchen shelf` (3)  
Next to the box stood a cookbook or two. The red and white checkered Better Homes and Gardens cookbook. The Betty Crocker cookbook, from a woman who, by the way, never existed. Betty Crocker was invented by a flour company in the nineteen twenties. But millions of women trusted her completely.

**65. [JULIA CHILD ARRIVES]** · fotos: `Julia Child` (3)  
And something new was happening in American cooking. In late nineteen sixty-one, a tall woman named Julia Child, with two co-authors, published a thick book called Mastering the Art of French Cooking. The next year, she appeared on public television in Boston, cooking an omelet. Soon she had her own show. Slowly, the American kitchen started to change.


## 24:26 · The things that came to the door

**66. [THE MILKMAN'S BOX]** · fotos: `milk box porch milk delivery` (3)  
Here's something younger people find hard to believe. In nineteen sixty-two, a lot of what filled the kitchen came to the back door by itself. The milkman came several mornings a week, leaving bottles in a metal or insulated box on the step, and in cold weather, the cream would freeze and push the cap right up out of the bottle.

**67. [THE FULLER BRUSH MAN]** · fotos: `Fuller Brush salesman` (3)  
The bread man came in some towns, and the egg man. The Fuller Brush man came with his case of brushes and cleaning products, and always had a little free gift for the lady of the house. And in many neighborhoods, a truck came around selling fruit and vegetables right off the back.

**68. [SOMEBODY WASHED, SOMEBODY DRIED]** · fotos: `washing dishes kitchen sink vintage` (3)  
The dishes, by the way, were washed by hand. Automatic dishwashers existed, but in nineteen sixty-two only a small share of American homes had one. So after dinner, somebody washed and somebody dried, standing side by side at the sink. Many of the best conversations of your childhood happened right there, over a dish towel.

**69. [THANKSGIVING]** · fotos: `Thanksgiving turkey dinner vintage` (3)  
Then there were the holidays, when the kitchen became the center of the universe. Thanksgiving, when the turkey went into the oven before dawn, and the windows fogged up from all the pots on the stove. Every burner was in use, every bowl was full, and the women of the family worked shoulder to shoulder while the men watched football in the next room.

**70. [THE KIDS' TABLE]** · fotos: `family holiday dinner table` (3)  
And at those big dinners, the children sat at the kids' table. A card table, set up in the kitchen or the corner of the dining room, with a mismatched tablecloth. You spent years trying to graduate to the grown-up table. And then, one year, you finally did, and somehow it was never quite as much fun.

**71. [THE WINDOW OVER THE SINK]** · fotos: `kitchen window sink plant` (3)  
Above the sink, there was a window. And on the windowsill, a little radio, a plant in a pot, and maybe a ceramic figurine. Your mother looked out of that window a thousand times a day, watching you play in the yard, waiting for your father's car to turn into the driveway.

**72. [THE KITCHEN RADIO]** · fotos: `vintage tabletop radio` (3)  
That little radio kept her company all day. The morning news. The farm report in rural towns. Music, soap operas that had moved over from radio to television but still played in some places, and a voice like Paul Harvey's at lunchtime, telling her the rest of the story. The kitchen was never truly quiet.

**73. [UNDER THE SINK]** · fotos: `vintage cleaning products` (3)  
Under the sink sat the cleaning supplies. A can of scouring powder. A box of soap flakes. Steel wool pads. A bottle of bleach. And the brown paper grocery bags, folded and saved, because they were useful for everything. Covering school books. Lining the garbage can. Draining the bacon.

**74. [THE STAMP BOOKS]** · fotos: `trading stamps books vintage` (3)  
And somewhere in that kitchen, in a drawer or a cupboard, were the trading stamp books. Your mother collected the stamps at the grocery store and the gas station, and on rainy afternoons, you licked them and pasted them in. When enough books were full, you went to the redemption center and traded them for a lamp, or a set of glasses, or a new toaster.


## 27:56 · The heart of the house

**75. [THE KITCHEN CLOCK]** · fotos: `vintage kitchen wall clock` (3)  
On the wall, above the doorway, ticked the kitchen clock. Maybe a sunburst. Maybe a cat whose tail swung back and forth and whose eyes moved with every second. That clock ran the house. When it said six, dinner was on the table. When it said eight, children went to bed.
