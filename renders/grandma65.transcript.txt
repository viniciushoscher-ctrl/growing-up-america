Sweetheart, the fancy wreaths in the catalogs cost a hundred dollars and up. My mother made ours every year for about fifty cents, with branches from the fencerow, a coat hanger, some string, and a red bow. And every December, a neighbor would knock on our door and ask where she bought it. She'd smile and say, Oh, I just threw it together. It took her a whole afternoon.

So tonight I'm giving you my mother's way to make a fresh Christmas wreath that looks expensive, for about five dollars, and the six mistakes that make a homemade wreath look cheap and thin. And stay with me, because the last trick is how to make it last fresh until New Year's, and it uses something from your bathroom cabinet.

The Saturday after Thanksgiving, my father took Danny and me out to Grandma Pearl's farm with a pair of pruning shears and a bushel basket. We cut cedar from the fencerow, white pine from the windbreak, and a few branches of holly from the bush by the barn. And we picked up pinecones from under the big spruce. My hands smelled like Christmas for a week.

Then my mother spread newspapers all over the kitchen table, and made the wreath while the radio played. Danny and I handed her the branches, one at a time. She said a wreath is a circle with no beginning and no end, like the love of God and the love of a family. In a minute I'll show you how she built it, starting with the frame.

Here's what you need, honey. A wire wreath frame, about fourteen inches across. You can buy one for two dollars, or bend a wire coat hanger into a circle, the way my mother did. A spool of green floral wire, or thin green string. A pair of pruning shears. Gloves. And a big pile of evergreen branches, more than you think. Two big grocery bags full.

For the greens, mix three or four kinds. Cedar for its soft, lacy look. White pine for long, soft needles. Fir or spruce for the deep green and the smell. Boxwood for a darker, finer texture. And a few accents, like holly berries, pinecones, dried orange slices, or eucalyptus. You can get all of it free from the bottom branches the Christmas tree lot trims off.

And wear gloves, honey, and old clothes. Fresh evergreens are full of sticky sap that gets on your hands and won't wash off with soap. Here's my mother's trick for that, too. Rub a little butter or cooking oil on your hands, and the sap slides right off. Then wash with soap. And put down plenty of newspaper, because you'll be finding needles in the kitchen until Easter.

Mistake number one. Too few branches. A thin wreath with the wire frame showing through looks cheap, like a bald spot. A wreath should be full, fat and round, at least five or six inches thick. That's why you need two bags of greens. When you think it's full enough, add one more round. My mother always said, a wreath is like a hug. A thin hug isn't worth much.

Step one, and here's mistake number two. Attaching the branches one at a time. It takes forever, and it looks stiff and flat. Instead, make little bundles. Cut the branches into pieces about six to eight inches long. Then gather three or four different pieces into a small bunch, a little cedar, a little pine, a little fir, like a little bouquet. Make about thirty of them.

Then lay one bundle on the frame, and wrap the floral wire tightly around the stems two or three times. Don't cut the wire. Lay the next bundle so it overlaps the first one by about half, covering the stems and the wire. Wrap it tight. Keep going, all the way around the circle, always in the same direction, like shingles on a roof.

If this is helping you, honey, tap the like button for me. It helps Grandma more than you know. And here's mistake number three. All the bundles pointing straight out the same way. Angle them a little. Some pointing toward the outside edge, some toward the inside, some straight up. That's what makes a wreath look round and full and natural, instead of flat like a dinner plate.

When you get all the way around, tuck the stems of the last bundle under the leaves of the first one, so you can't see where it starts or ends. A circle with no beginning and no end, like my mother said. Then tie off the wire tight on the back. Turn it over and make a little loop of wire at the top for hanging.

So far, two bags of mixed greens, little bundles, overlapping like shingles, angled out, and a hidden ending. Now the bow, and mistake number four. A small, limp bow. A wreath needs a big, full bow, with long tails. Use wide wired ribbon, at least two and a half inches, so it holds its shape. Velvet or plaid looks the richest.

Here's how my mother made a big bow. Make a loop, pinch it in the middle. Make another loop on the other side, pinch it. Keep going, back and forth, three loops on each side, all pinched together in the middle. Then wrap a short piece of wire around the pinch, twist it tight, and cover the middle with a small loop of ribbon. Leave two long tails, cut in a V.

And put the bow at the bottom, sweetheart, or at the bottom left, not always at the top. A bow at the bottom looks graceful and lets the tails hang down. Or skip the bow, and make a big cluster of pinecones and berries at the bottom instead. My mother changed it every year, and that's how the neighbors knew it was homemade and not from a catalog.

Mistake number five. Too much stuff stuck in the wreath. Ornaments, fake birds, glitter, little signs, all crammed in. It hides all the beautiful greens. Pick two or three accents, and group them in clusters of three. Three pinecones together here, three sprigs of berries there. Clusters look planned and natural. Things spread evenly all over look like polka dots.

To attach a pinecone, honey, wrap a piece of floral wire around its bottom row of scales, twist it, and leave two long wire ends. Then push the ends through the wreath and twist them together on the back. Dried orange slices and cinnamon sticks attach the same way. My mother wired a few red apples into hers. They smelled wonderful, and the birds ate them by New Year's.

And now the last mistake, the one I promised, and the trick from your bathroom cabinet. Mistake number six is letting the wreath dry out. A fresh wreath on a sunny door, or inside by the heat, turns brown and drops needles in two weeks. Before you hang it, soak the whole wreath in a bathtub of cold water overnight. The branches drink up the water.

Then, after it dries, spray it all over with a little hairspray, from the bathroom cabinet. It seals in the moisture and keeps the needles from falling. That was my mother's secret, honey. People who sell wreaths use a spray called anti-desiccant, but cheap hairspray works almost as well. And mist it with plain water every few days, especially if it's indoors.

And hang it in the right place, sweetheart. Outside on a shaded door, a fresh wreath lasts six weeks or more in the cold. Inside, keep it away from fireplaces, heater vents and sunny windows. And never near candles. Dry greens catch fire in a second. My mother hung hers on the front door, with a smaller one in the kitchen window.

Once you know how, you can make one for every door in the house. Or tiny ones on a coat hanger for the windows. Or a long swag instead, with the bundles wired onto a long stick. And they make a beautiful Christmas gift. A fresh homemade wreath for an elderly neighbor, who can't get out to buy one, is the kind of gift that makes people cry.

After my husband passed, the first Christmas, I couldn't make myself do anything. And on the first of December, my granddaughter showed up with a wreath she'd made herself, crooked and a little thin, with a lopsided bow. She'd learned it from watching me for twenty years. I hung it on my front door, and I've never been so proud of a crooked wreath in my life.

So one more time. A wire frame or a coat hanger. Two bags of mixed greens. Little bundles of three or four kinds. Wire them on overlapping, like shingles, and angle them. A big wired bow at the bottom. A few accents in clusters of three. Soak it overnight, and a little hairspray. That's my mother's fifty-cent wreath, the one the neighbors asked about.
