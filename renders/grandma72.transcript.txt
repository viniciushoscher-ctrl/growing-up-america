Coconut cake was a Southern specialty first, honey. In the eighteen hundreds, the cooks of Charleston and Savannah made it for weddings and holidays. Here's what you need for the cake. Three cups of cake flour, not all-purpose. One tablespoon of baking powder and half a teaspoon of salt. One cup of soft butter. Two cups of sugar. Five egg whites, at room temperature. One cup of canned coconut milk, well stirred. One teaspoon of vanilla, and one teaspoon of coconut extract if you have it.

And here's mistake number one. Using all-purpose flour and whole eggs. All-purpose flour makes a tough, heavy cake. Cake flour makes it soft and tender, like a cloud. And whole eggs turn the cake yellow. Egg whites keep it snow white inside, which is the whole point of a white Christmas cake. Save the yolks for eggnog or custard. Nothing goes to waste.

And mistake number two. Using regular milk. Regular milk is fine, but canned coconut milk gives you coconut flavor all the way through the cake, not just on the outside. Grandma Pearl used the coconut water from her fresh coconuts. Coconut milk in a can is the closest thing, and it's about two dollars.

Heat your oven to three hundred fifty. Grease three eight-inch round pans, line the bottoms with a circle of wax paper or parchment, and grease and flour them again. Whisk the flour, baking powder and salt together in one bowl. Now beat the soft butter and sugar together for a full five minutes, until it's pale and fluffy. Don't skip that. That's where the lightness comes from.

Add the egg whites one at a time, beating after each. Add the vanilla and coconut extract. Now add the flour in three parts, alternating with the coconut milk in two parts, starting and ending with flour. Mix on low, only until you can't see any more flour. That's mistake number three, honey. Overmixing makes a tough, rubbery cake.

And here's mistake number four. Overbaking. Most people leave a cake in until the toothpick comes out completely dry, and by then, the cake is already drying out. A few moist crumbs are perfect. The cake keeps cooking for a few minutes after it comes out of the oven. Set a timer for twenty-two minutes, and check it then.

Now the secret I promised you, the thing Grandma Pearl did the night before. She brushed every layer with a simple syrup, while the cake was still a little warm. Half a cup of sugar and half a cup of coconut milk, heated just until the sugar melts. She brushed it on with a pastry brush, and the cake drank it all up. Then she wrapped each layer in wax paper overnight.

That's why her cake was the moistest in Richland County. The syrup soaks in, and overnight the crumb gets soft and rich, and it stays that way for days. And a cold, rested cake is so much easier to frost, honey. It doesn't crumble, and it doesn't slide. Bakeries do this, and now you know their secret.

Grandma Pearl used a seven-minute frosting, but I'll give you the easy one I've used for forty years. Beat eight ounces of soft cream cheese with half a cup of soft butter until smooth. Add four cups of powdered sugar, a pinch of salt, a teaspoon of vanilla, and half a teaspoon of coconut extract. Beat until fluffy and white.

Put the first layer on your cake stand, and spread a thick layer of frosting on top. Sprinkle on a big handful of coconut. Repeat with the second layer, then the third. Now frost the top and sides with a thin coat first, to catch the crumbs, and chill it for twenty minutes. Then put on the second, thick coat. That's the crumb coat trick, and it keeps the white frosting perfectly white.

Now mistake number five. Not using enough coconut. You need about three cups of sweetened flaked coconut.
